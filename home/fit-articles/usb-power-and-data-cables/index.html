USB power and data cables

USB ports are everywhere. Some USB cables are for power only and others offer both power and data transfers. Apple products can use proprietary USB ports; see their specs on Apple's website: https://support.apple.com/en-us/109523

The Universal Serial Bus (USB 1.1) standard, released in 1998, was conceived to replace the tangle of proprietary serial and parallel ports and cables with a single, universal connector with a theoretical speed of 12 Mbps and focused primarily on convenience, meaning you could plug devices in and start using them immediately without having to restart your computer. The first major leap came with USB 2.0 (theoretically 480 Megabytes per second (Mbps) in 2000, 40 times faster that of USB 1.1 which established USB 2 as a legitimate high-speed data interface for external storage and flash drives and to recharge batteries in USB devices. USB standards are designed for backward compatibility to ensure that new USB ports  can still work with USB ports on older devices. For example, all modern USB ports include a separate set of internal wires dedicated to older USB 2.0 connections. This is why an old flash drive will still plug into a brand-new port and work, though its speed will be limited to the slower 480 Mbps rate of the USB 2.0 device itself. However, in this particular example a USB adapter from USB-A to USB-C would also be necessary.

 Subsequent versions, starting with USB 3.0 (SuperSpeed) in 2008, ushered in a massive increase in data bandwidth, jumping to 5 Gbps and introducing dedicated lanes for simultaneous two-way data flow. The modern era of USB 3.2 and USB4 has quadrupled speeds up to 40 Gbps and beyond, fundamentally shifting USB's role. Power Delivery (PD)—a smart charging protocol that allows cables and ports to dynamically negotiate higher voltage and wattage—allows a single cable to handle high-resolution video, massive files, and heavy-duty power up to 240W simultaneously.

Where Thunderbolt Fits In: Originally created by Intel and Apple as a high-end connection for professionals, Thunderbolt technology was later shared with the USB organization to help build modern USB4. Because of this, Thunderbolt and USB-C are now close cousins: a Thunderbolt cable can plug into a standard USB-C port (and vice versa), automatically adjusting to the best speed your devices support.

A critical distinction today exists between full-featured data cables and cheaper, "charge-only" power cables. While they may look identical externally (especially USB Type-C), cheaper charging cables often leave out the internal wires needed to actually send files or video signals. A bargain cable might charge your phone slowly, but refuse to move your photos to your computer. Users must closely check the packaging for logos or explicit labeling that indicates speed standards (e.g., "5 Gbps," "USB4 40Gbps") and power capacity (e.g., "PD 100W") to guarantee the cable supports both the required speed and power level for their device.
